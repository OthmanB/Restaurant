\begin{abstract}
This document proposes a parametric model for restaurant demand, revenue, expenses, and profit. The purpose is not to replace data analysis or machine learning, but to provide a transparent analytical reference framework whose assumptions are explicit and whose parameters can later be calibrated against observations. The presentation deliberately starts from the most general formulation, then introduces practical simplifications that keep the model analytically manageable, and finally ends with worked examples. The resulting theory is meant to be strong enough to guide later quantitative developments.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The goal of these notes is to provide an analytical model for the expected financial behavior of a restaurant, starting from a description of the accessible population, the temporal structure of demand, the menu, and the expense model.

The present document does not use machine learning for prediction. Instead, it describes a structured parametric model whose ingredients can later be estimated, challenged, or refined from data. This is useful for early-stage exploration because it exposes the points where the business is most sensitive: catchment size, competition, service timing, basket composition, and recurring costs. If proven sufficient, this can also serve as a base for training ML predicive models such that one may get forecasts in near real time.

The document follows three recurring levels. A general form, with weak assumptions and broad interpretability.
Follows a practical reference model, chosen so that the model remains analytically and numerically manageable. Then we present worked examples, used as a pedagogical case first and as a richer near-implementation case second.

The model aims at expected values over a planning horizon. It is therefore suited to scenario analysis, parameter sensitivity, and the construction of reproducible examples. It is not a stochastic queueing model, and it does not attempt to predict short-term shocks, weather anomalies, or operational disruptions unless such effects are introduced explicitly as extra factors.

The reference theory intentionally remains compact. It ignores many second-order effects, such as social-network feedback, inventory constraints, dynamic pricing, endogenous waiting times, and stockout behavior. These are natural future extensions rather than part of the reference model.

\subsection{General population reservoir and customer flow}
\label{sec:population-general}

Let \( \mathcal{G} = \{l,w,tr\} \) denote the set of population groups: local residents, workers or students, and transit populations. The most general decomposition of the accessible population is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Ntot-general}
    N_{tot}(t) = \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} N_g(t).
\end{equation}

The total population reservoir is not yet the customer flow. A second layer is needed to describe how the accessible population converts into arrivals. We therefore introduce a group-dependent attractiveness factor \( A_g(t) \), and a general temporal modulation \( M_g(t) \) with units of inverse time. The expected customer arrival rate is then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Nc-general}
    N_c(t) = \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} N_g(t) A_g(t) M_g(t).
\end{equation}

This distinction is essential. The quantities \( N_g(t) \) and \( N_{tot}(t) \) are counts of potentially reachable people, whereas \( N_c(t) \) is a rate of arrivals. The model is easier to interpret when this distinction is made explicit from the outset.

\paragraph{Factorized attractiveness.}
In the general theory, attractiveness should not be treated as a single opaque scalar. It is more informative to write it as a product of interpretable factors,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:A-factorized}
    A_g(t) = \prod_{m=1}^{M_g} A_g^{(m)}(t), \qquad 0 \leq A_g^{(m)}(t) \leq 1.
\end{equation}
Typical components may encode competition, visibility, offer fit, affordability, reputation, and convenience. The main simplification is to assume approximate orthogonality between these factors so that the product remains compact. In pedagogical examples or first implementations, this factorized expression can be reduced to a single effective factor \( A_g \) or even to a single shared \( A \).

\paragraph{Competition limit.}
If the focal restaurant competes with \( n_r \) comparable restaurants inside the same catchment area, and if \( n_r \) counts competitors only, then a conservative equal-quality approximation is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:A-competition}
    A_g^{(\mathrm{comp})} \simeq \frac{1}{n_r + 1}.
\end{equation}
This relation is not a law; it is a limiting-case approximation obtained when the restaurants are almost interchangeable and the available customer demand is divided roughly evenly among them.

\paragraph{Reference model.}
For the reference model, we assume that the factorized attractiveness terms vary slowly enough to be treated as time-constant effective parameters over the planning horizon. Time-varying preference shocks can still be absorbed into the more general modulation \( M_g(t) \) if needed. In the reference model, the temporal modulation is further factorized into daily, weekly, and yearly components,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Nc-separable}
    N_c(t) = \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}} N_g(t) A_g D_g(\tau_d) W_g(\tau_w) Y_g(\tau_y),
\end{equation}
where \( \tau_d = t \bmod 24\,\mathrm{h} \), \( \tau_w = t \bmod 7\,\mathrm{d} \), and \( \tau_y = t \bmod 1\,\mathrm{y} \). When parameter count must be reduced, one may collapse this to a shared structure,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Nc-baseline}
    N_c(t) = A N_{tot}(t) D(\tau_d) W(\tau_w) Y(\tau_y).
\end{equation}
This compact form is convenient, but the document keeps the group-dependent version as the conceptual reference.

\subsection{Spatial influence and effective area}
\label{sec:spatial}

The number of residents, workers, or transit users that belong to the accessible reservoir depends on space. The most general object is an anisotropic influence kernel \( I_g(r,\theta) \), which depends on radial distance, direction, terrain, road structure, and effective mobility.

For the living population, the general expression is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Nliving-general}
    N_l = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\infty} \rho_l(r,\theta) I_l(r,\theta) r \, dr \, d\theta,
\end{equation}
where \( \rho_l(r,\theta) \) is the local residential density. In the reference model we now assume a uniform local density \( \rho_l \) and an isotropic kernel \( I_l(r,\theta) = I_l(r) \).

\paragraph{Gaussian reference kernel.}
The reference kernel is chosen to be Gaussian and peak-normalized,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kernel-gaussian}
    I_l(r) = \exp\left(-\frac{r^2}{2 R_{\mathrm{eff},l}^2}\right), \qquad I_l(0) = 1.
\end{equation}
It is important not to confuse peak normalization with integral normalization. Equation \ref{eq:kernel-gaussian} is not a probability density over space; it is a dimensionless reach kernel whose peak value is one.

The corresponding effective area is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Seff}
    S_{\mathrm{eff},l} = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\infty} I_l(r) r \, dr \, d\theta = 2\pi R_{\mathrm{eff},l}^2.
\end{equation}
Therefore the living population reservoir becomes
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Nliving-baseline}
    N_l = \rho_l S_{\mathrm{eff},l} = 2\pi R_{\mathrm{eff},l}^2 \rho_l.
\end{equation}
In the reference model, local residents are treated as a time-invariant reservoir to first order, so \( N_l(t) \approx N_l \) over the planning horizon unless a separate residential time modulation is introduced. If a probability-normalized kernel is preferred, one may instead use \( I_l / S_{\mathrm{eff},l} \), but then the interpretation of the integral changes and an external area scale must be reintroduced elsewhere. For the present document, peak normalization is retained for the spatial kernel.

\paragraph{Working population.}
A working or studying site is better modeled as a discrete reservoir. Let \( N_{w,i}(t) \) be the total number of workers or students at site \( i \), and let \( N^{(0)}_{w,i}(t) \) denote the subset already counted among the local residents. The practical reference form is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Nworking}
    N_w(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{n_w} \left(N_{w,i}(t) - N^{(0)}_{w,i}(t)\right) I_w(r_i).
\end{equation}
This is a compact de-duplication rule: residents who also work nearby are not double-counted when constructing the accessible reservoir. In the practical study cases, we write \( N^{(0)}_{w,i}(t) = \phi_i N_{w,i}(t) \), where \( \phi_i \in [0,1] \) is the commute-overlap fraction of site \( i \). More detailed versions could replace \( \phi_i \) by an overlap integral between residential density and work-site catchment structure.

\paragraph{Transit population.}
Transit demand deserves a separate term in the general theory. It may arise from tourists, transport hubs, beaches, or other non-resident flows. In a full model one would infer \( N_{tr}(t) \) from local pedestrian flux or transport exposure. In the reference study case below, the clean limit \( N_{tr}(t)=0 \) is kept first, and a small transit extension is discussed afterward.

\paragraph{Alternative kernels.}
The Gaussian reference kernel is not the only possible choice. An exponential kernel has a longer tail and may better reflect corridors or sparse catchments; a hard cutoff is easy to explain but introduces a non-smooth boundary. Figure \ref{fig:spatial-concepts} is therefore not merely decorative: it clarifies why the Gaussian choice is retained in the reference formulation while keeping alternatives visible for later refinements.

\subsection{Daily, weekly, and yearly modulation}
\label{sec:temporal}

Demand varies across several recurring timescales. The most general object is a non-separable modulation \( M_g(t) \) in equation \ref{eq:Nc-general}. This is conceptually clean but often too rich for analytical work. The reference approximation is therefore the separable form of equation \ref{eq:Nc-separable}.

\paragraph{Daily kernel.}
The daily component carries the rate interpretation in the reference model. We define it so that its integral over one day yields an expected number of visits per accessible person,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:D-normalization}
    \int_0^{24\,\mathrm{h}} D_g(\tau_d) \, d\tau_d = \nu_g,
\end{equation}
where \( \nu_g \) is the expected number of daily meal opportunities or visits for group \( g \). A practical parametric form is a sum of Gaussian meal peaks,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:D-gaussian}
    D_g(\tau_d) = \frac{\nu_g}{Z_g} \sum_{m=1}^{M_g^{(\mathrm{meal})}} a_{g,m} \exp\left(-\frac{(\tau_d - \mu_{g,m})^2}{2 \sigma_{g,m}^2}\right),
\end{equation}
with normalization constant
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:D-Z}
    Z_g = \int_0^{24\,\mathrm{h}} \sum_{m=1}^{M_g^{(\mathrm{meal})}} a_{g,m} \exp\left(-\frac{(\tau_d - \mu_{g,m})^2}{2 \sigma_{g,m}^2}\right) d\tau_d.
\end{equation}
This choice preserves a simple analytical description of repeated meal peaks while keeping the daily structure explicit.

\paragraph{Weekly and yearly envelopes.}
The weekly and yearly components should modulate the daily kernel without changing its physical units. A coherent reference convention is therefore
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:W-normalization}
    \frac{1}{T_w} \int_0^{T_w} W_g(\tau_w) \, d\tau_w = 1, \qquad T_w = 7\,\mathrm{d},
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Y-normalization}
    \frac{1}{T_y} \int_0^{T_y} Y_g(\tau_y) \, d\tau_y = 1, \qquad T_y = 365\,\mathrm{d}.
\end{equation}
With this convention, \( W_g \) and \( Y_g \) are dimensionless envelopes and \( D_g \) retains the full rate interpretation.

A practical weekly envelope can be written as day-of-week weights renormalized to unit mean,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:W-example}
    W_g(\tau_w) = \frac{\omega_{g,d(\tau_w)}}{\frac{1}{7} \sum_{k=1}^{7} \omega_{g,k}},
\end{equation}
where \( d(\tau_w) \) is the day-of-week index. Such a weekly modulation can be constructed by repeating the daily template and weighting the days of the week appropriately.

A simple yearly envelope is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Y-example}
    Y_g(\tau_y) = A_{y,g} - D_{y,g} \cos\left(\frac{2\pi \tau_y}{P_g} + \phi_g\right), \qquad A_{y,g} > D_{y,g} \ge 0.
\end{equation}
This is intentionally simple. Holidays, events, or weather shocks can later be added as local pulses or exogenous corrections on top of this yearly envelope.

\section{Menu, baskets, and expected spend}
\label{sec:menu}

The menu is not only a list of prices. It is a time-dependent random object because basket composition changes across services and across customer groups. The correct starting point is therefore the basket distribution itself.

\subsection{General basket model}
\label{sec:menu-general}

Let \( L \) index menu items and let \( \mathbf{n} = (n_L)_L \) denote the random basket associated with one arriving customer at time \( t \). The most general object is the conditional basket distribution \( \Pi(\mathbf{n}|t) \), with \( n_L \) in \( \mathbb{N} \). The expected spend per customer at time \( t \) is then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pbar-general}
    \bar{p}(t) = \sum_{\mathbf{n}} \Pi(\mathbf{n}|t) \sum_L n_L p_L,
\end{equation}
and the expected production cost per customer is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cbar-general}
    \bar{c}(t) = \sum_{\mathbf{n}} \Pi(\mathbf{n}|t) \sum_L n_L c_L.
\end{equation}
This formulation keeps multi-item purchases in the theory from the outset. It is therefore the appropriate reference formulation for the present document.

\subsection{Practical approximation used for analysis}
\label{sec:menu-practical}

The full basket distribution is usually too large to parameterize directly. A practical reduction is to work with per-item expected multiplicities or, under a stronger assumption, with at-most-one-unit Bernoulli probabilities. Let \( \pi_L(t) \) denote the probability that one unit of item \( L \) is included in the basket at time \( t \). Under the at-most-one-unit approximation,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pbar-practical}
    \bar{p}(t) = \sum_L p_L \pi_L(t), \qquad \bar{c}(t) = \sum_L c_L \pi_L(t).
\end{equation}
This reduced description preserves a tractable connection between service-dependent choice probabilities and expected monetary flows.

A service-specific parameterization can be written as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pi-service}
    \pi_L(t) = q_L s_{\sigma(L)}(\tau_d),
\end{equation}
where \( q_L \) is an item-level inclusion probability over the day, \( \sigma(L) \) identifies the relevant service, and \( s_{\sigma} \) is a normalized service-time profile satisfying
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:service-profile}
    \int_0^{24\,\mathrm{h}} s_{\sigma}(\tau_d) \, d\tau_d = 1.
\end{equation}
The general multi-item theory is therefore reduced to a manageable special case without losing interpretability.

\section{Revenue, expenses, and profit}
\label{sec:revenues-expenses}

\subsection{Expected revenue}
\label{sec:revenues}

With the notation above, the expected revenue rate is simply the customer arrival rate multiplied by the expected spend per customer,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:revenue-rate}
    r(t) = N_c(t) \bar{p}(t).
\end{equation}
The expected revenue over a horizon \( \Delta t \) is therefore
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:revenue-total}
    \mathcal{R}(\Delta t) = \int_0^{\Delta t} N_c(t) \bar{p}(t) \, dt.
\end{equation}
This is a proper equality, not a proportionality. The normalization choices are carried by \( D, W, Y \) and by the basket model itself; there is no missing multiplicative constant.

A discrete-time approximation with step \( \delta t \) is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:revenue-discrete}
    \mathcal{R}(\Delta t) \approx \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} N_c(t_n) \bar{p}(t_n) \delta t,
\end{equation}
where \( t_n = n \delta t \) and \( N \delta t = \Delta t \). This provides a natural discrete approximation for numerical evaluation.

\subsection{Production cost and payment fees}
\label{sec:costs-variable}

The expected production-cost rate is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Eprod}
    e_{\mathrm{prod}}(t) = N_c(t) \bar{c}(t),
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Eprod-total}
    E_{\mathrm{prod}}(\Delta t) = \int_0^{\Delta t} N_c(t) \bar{c}(t) \, dt.
\end{equation}

If several payment methods are available, with method probabilities \( \alpha_m(t) \) and fee fractions \( f_m \), the expected fee factor is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fee-factor}
    \bar{f}(t) = \sum_m \alpha_m(t) f_m,
\end{equation}
and the corresponding fee expense is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Efees}
    E_{\mathrm{fees}}(\Delta t) = \int_0^{\Delta t} N_c(t) \bar{p}(t) \bar{f}(t) \, dt.
\end{equation}
The deterministic expectation of equation \ref{eq:Efees} is the natural analytical object. If desired, one may later replace it with a stochastic simulation of payment choices.

\subsection{Recurring, staff, and fixed costs}
\label{sec:costs-fixed}

Recurring costs are most naturally expressed as rates. Let \( r_i \) be the recurring expense rate associated with item \( i \) (rent, software, utilities, marketing, accounting, and so on). Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Erec}
    E_{\mathrm{rec}}(\Delta t) = \sum_i r_i \Delta t.
\end{equation}

Staff costs can be written either from hourly schedules or from average staffing rates. If staff member \( j \) is paid \( w_j \) per hour and works \( h_j(\Delta t) \) hours during the horizon,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Estaff}
    E_{\mathrm{staff}}(\Delta t) = \sum_j w_j h_j(\Delta t).
\end{equation}

The most general model also includes fixed or one-time costs. If \( F_k \) denotes the one-time expense associated with event \( k \), then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Efix}
    E_{\mathrm{fix}}(\Delta t) = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}(\Delta t)} F_k,
\end{equation}
where \( \mathcal{K}(\Delta t) \) is the set of fixed-cost events occurring within the horizon. A reduced operating model may omit this term intentionally, but the general model should keep it explicit.

The total expense model is therefore
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Etot}
    E_{\mathrm{tot}}(\Delta t) = E_{\mathrm{prod}}(\Delta t) + E_{\mathrm{fees}}(\Delta t) + E_{\mathrm{rec}}(\Delta t) + E_{\mathrm{staff}}(\Delta t) + E_{\mathrm{fix}}(\Delta t),
\end{equation}
and expected income / profit is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:profit}
    \mathcal{I}(\Delta t) = \mathcal{R}(\Delta t) - E_{\mathrm{tot}}(\Delta t).
\end{equation}

\section{Discussion and practical limits}
\label{sec:discussion}

The revised model is intentionally layered. The general form keeps the conceptual objects clear: population reservoir, conversion structure, temporal modulation, basket model, and expense decomposition. The reference model then chooses a Gaussian spatial kernel, a separable daily-weekly-yearly structure, and a compact basket approximation because these assumptions remain analytically tractable while preserving the main mechanisms of interest.

Several limitations remain explicit. The separable temporal structure remains an approximation, and holidays, events, or weather shocks may introduce couplings between timescales that are absent from the reference formulation. The factorization of attractiveness likewise assumes an approximate orthogonality between several interpretable effects; this may cease to be adequate when competition, accessibility, and customer preferences become strongly dependent on one another. The practical menu approximation also ignores within-basket correlations unless the full basket distribution is retained. Finally, transit demand remains strongly location-dependent, and the reduced operating view may omit one-time costs when attention is restricted to short-run operation, even though the general expense equation keeps them explicitly.

These limitations are not flaws of presentation. They indicate the directions in which the theoretical framework can be refined once additional data, constraints, or modeling priorities are introduced.

\section{Appendix A: analytical summary for numerical realization}
\label{sec:appendix-implementation}

Although the present article is purely theoretical, it is useful to isolate the analytical blocks that any later numerical realization would have to reproduce. The first block concerns the spatial sector, namely the evaluation of the Gaussian influence kernel, its associated effective area, and the construction of the living and working reservoirs. The second block concerns temporal modulation, that is, the normalization of the daily meal-peak superposition together with the weekly and yearly envelopes. The third block concerns the basket description, where the general multi-item formulation may be reduced to expected item-level contributions when a simpler approximation is desired. The fourth block concerns the aggregation stage, in which arrival rates, expected spend, and expense rates are integrated over the chosen horizon. In that sense, the theory naturally decomposes into a spatial block, a temporal block, a basket block, and an aggregation block, each of which can later be translated into a concrete numerical procedure without modifying the equations themselves.
